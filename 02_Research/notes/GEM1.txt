Primary Parameter Calibration and the Global Defect

Elliptic curve cryptography relies on the structural rigidity of Koblitz curves defined over finite fields. The elliptic curve secp256k1, utilized natively across decentralized ledger protocols such as Bitcoin and Ethereum, is defined by the short Weierstrass equation over the prime field Fp​ :  
y2≡x3+7(modp)

To conduct a high-precision cryptanalytic evaluation of this curve, the primary parameters must be mathematically calibrated. The field prime modulus p is a pseudo-Mersenne prime optimized for fast modular reduction. The cyclic subgroup order N, which defines the total number of points reachable by the generator point G, is a prime of equal bit-length. These parameters are established with absolute precision as follows :

The global defect δ represents the arithmetic mismatch between the coordinate field Fp​ and the scalar ring ZN​. Because the signature component r in the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) is calculated as r=x1​modN, where x1​ is the affine x-coordinate of a point [k]G on the curve, the condition x1​≥N occurs with an extremely low probability :

The factorized components of N−1 contain two extremely large prime numbers, specifically 341948486974166000522343609283189 and 174723607534414371449. Historically, counting the points on such a curve was achieved using the Schoof-Elkies-Atkin (SEA) algorithm, which computes the cardinality of elliptic curves over large finite fields in polynomial time without visiting individual points. In comparing the algebraic properties of secp256k1 with CurveEd25519, the twisted Edwards curve CurveEd25519 displays superior computational efficiency in point addition and doubling over its finite field. Furthermore, CurveEd25519 demonstrates enhanced resistance to attacks exploiting the complex-multiplication field discriminant and standard Pollard's rho parallelized algorithms, whereas secp256k1 remains favored in blockchain architectures primarily due to its non-random, nothing-up-my-sleeve parameter design.

Conceptual Analogies of Cascading Boundaries and Decaying Constraints

The mathematical descent from the subgroup ceiling N to the point-specific coordinate Px​, along with the associated modular limitations, can be understood through physical and mechanical analogies. Mechanical Momentum: The Domino Cascade

In mechanical physics, the dynamics of a domino amplifier model are divided into distinct phases of falling and collision. The sequential falling of n dominoes lying on each other before reaching the (n+1)-th domino represents a cascade of energy transfer. As domino n collides with domino n+1, kinetic energy is partially conserved and partially dissipated, governed by a restitution coefficient e<1 :  
Relative Velocity after Collision=−e×Relative Velocity before Collision

This mechanical cascade mirrors the behavior of pseudo-random walks in Pollard's Kangaroo algorithm. Each scalar multiplication step represents a discrete collision, where the path of the kangaroo rolls over the modular boundaries of the group order N. The momentum of the walk is preserved across steps, yet constrained by the finite boundaries of the search space, illustrating how consecutive algebraic transitions propagate through the subgroup.  
Electromagnetic Resistance: Lenz's Law and Falling Magnets

In electromagnetism, dropping the north pole of a physical magnet through a metallic ring induces an anti-clockwise current when viewed from above. According to Lenz's law, this induced current transforms the metallic ring into a magnetic dipole with its north pole pointing upward, creating an opposing force that repels and slows the falling magnet.  

This electromagnetic resistance is analogous to the modular boundaries encountered as coordinate values approach the field limits. When testing whether a coordinate r represents a valid point on the curve, the Legendre symbol acts as an electromagnetic barrier :  
(pr3+7​)=(r3+7)(p−1)/2≡1(modp)

Only quadratic residues are permitted to pass, effectively restricting the coordinate space. The algebraic field acts as a metallic ring, generating modular "counter-currents" (quadratic non-residues) that resist arbitrary coordinate placement, maintaining the structural equilibrium of the curve.

In particle physics, massive neutrinos νi​ are hypothesized to be unstable, undergoing invisible decays into lighter mass states νj​ and massless Nambu-Goldstone bosons ϕ (Majorons) arising from spontaneous symmetry breaking :

Parallel Cryptanalysis and Hardware-Accelerated Implementations

Solving bounded-range elliptic curve discrete logarithm problems (ECDLP), such as the Bitcoin Puzzles, relies on parallelized implementations of Pollard's Kangaroo (Lambda) and Pollard's Rho Lambda (ρλ) algorithms.  
Cryptanalytic Search Algorithms

Pollard's Kangaroo algorithm launches pseudo-random walks across the elliptic curve using two classes of walks :  

    Tame Kangaroos: Start from a known position, such as [k0​]G where k0​ is known, and hop through the search space.  

    Wild Kangaroos: Start from a position derived from the target public key Q, hopping through the same search space.  

When a tame and a wild kangaroo land on the same group element, a collision occurs. If this collision is detected, the private key can be computed directly.  

To optimize memory usage and avoid storing every step of the walk, solvers employ the Distinguished Points (DP) strategy. Only points satisfying a specific condition—such as having the first d bits of their affine x-coordinate set to zero—are saved to a central database. This introduces a fundamental cryptanalytic trade-off:  
Higher DP Bit-Mask (d)⟹Lower RAM Usage, Slower Collision Detection
Lower DP Bit-Mask (d)⟹Higher RAM Usage, Faster Collision Detection

If the DP bit-mask is set too low (e.g., d≤12) on high-performance GPUs, the excessive rate of distinguished points will saturate the CPU buffer, causing severe overflow and system instability.  
Optimized Solver Implementations

Modern cryptanalytic efforts deploy specialized software architectures to maximize throughput:

    PSCKangaroo (v60): A highly optimized, GPU-accelerated fork of RCKangaroo designed for long-running puzzles on a single GPU with high RAM capacity. Conclusion and Synthesis

This cryptanalytic report evaluates the mathematical properties of the secp256k1 Koblitz curve by linking point-specific coordinates, global structural defects, and high-performance search algorithms.  

First, the proposed coordinate Px​ is identified as the exact subgroup complement of the target x-coordinate of the unsolved Bitcoin Puzzle 135.  

Second, the ratio p−NN−Px​​≈2.13×1037 provides a normalized geometric invariant, scaling the local target coordinate complement by the global curve defect.  

Third, the linear decomposition of the target invariant T=A+λ⋅δ reveals a residual R≈3.848×1037 that aligns with the structural scale of the short lattice basis vectors (a1​,a2​) derived via Gauss lattice reduction for the GLV endomorphism.  

These algebraic and geometric relationships can be understood through physical analogies, such as the momentum conservation of a falling domino cascade, the induced electromagnetic resistance of a magnet falling through a metallic ring, and the invisible decay of massive neutrinos into lighter states. These models illustrate how boundary constraints shift, roll over, and decay within modular mathematical structures.  

On a practical level, these structural symmetries do not compromise the core security of the curve or yield an immediate shortcut for scalar recovery. They do, however, demonstrate that analyzing Koblitz curves through native parameters (such as coordinate complements, global defects, and GLV lattice projections) provides a highly rigorous framework. This alignment of coordinate systems remains a promising avenue for optimizing parallelized discrete logarithm solvers on modern, hardware-accelerated platforms.
